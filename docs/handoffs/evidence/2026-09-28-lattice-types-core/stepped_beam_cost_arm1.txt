TWO MEMORY FIGURES, AND WHY THEY DIFFER. They are not in conflict and neither is
wrong. `ru_maxrss` (my column) is the kernel's high-water mark. Core's `peak_rss_mb`
is `max(every sampled rss_mb, every ru_maxrss)`, and on macOS its `rss_mb` is
**phys_footprint via task_info** — a different quantity that counts compressed pages —
so core's figure is systematically the larger. Core's own comment
(observability.cpp:205-235) documents exactly this. **Plan with CORE's number**: it is
the one core itself reads against `available_mb` for OOM diagnosis.

WALL TIMES ARE UPPER BOUNDS AND THE LOAD PROVES IT. load1 at the start of each
certificate: 11.05, then 88.55, then 179.93. The box was carrying other agents' work
throughout, and 179 on this machine is saturation. Memory is not load-dependent; the
seconds column is, and should be read as "no worse than".

★ THE DOMINANT COST DRIVER IS NOT THE CELL COUNT. It is the ratio of the longest strut
to the THINNEST radius in the whole plan, because the weld piece is 2 x the thinnest
radius and every strut is cut to it:

    500 cells  (bands 8, 9, 4, 4.5 mm)        piece 0.8024 mm   x5.0 subdivision
    2,000 cells (reaches the 2, 2.25 mm bands) piece 0.3796 mm   x6.0
    8,000 cells (same bands)                   piece 0.3796 mm   x5.7

One 2 mm cell anywhere in the plan halves the piece and so doubles the segment count
for every 9 mm strut in the part. A plan of only 8/9 mm cells is far cheaper than its
cell count suggests; a plan with one fine family is far dearer. Any forecast must key
on the finest family, not on the number of cells.

WHAT WAS REFUSED, AND WHY IT MATTERS (first attempt, same probe, slabs only)
Solid slabs at both ends with the plan between them and nothing else is REFUSED before
the solve starts:
    1 solid piece(s) totalling 6912 element(s) (50.000000% of the meshed solid) touch
    the rest of the part only at an edge or a corner and reach no support: they are
    hinges, not structure
beam_network.cpp:1121: "A component is real if a Dirichlet node sits on one of its
elements." Support is hex connectivity plus Dirichlet; THE BEAMS DO NOT COUNT. So a
lattice-only bridge between two solid regions cannot be certified by this instrument
at all, whatever the struts do — a constraint any future Stepped certificate inherits.
It does not affect a part whose lattice sits in a pocket inside a connected shell,
which is every real part and why organic certifies.

THE NO-LATTICE CONTROL: ASKED FOR, AND IT DOES NOT APPLY TO THIS ARRANGEMENT.
The control — same box and slabs, zero cells, expecting the load to find no path —
tests the SLABS-ONLY geometry, where the beams are the only bridge. That geometry is
refused before the solve (see the island rule below), so it could never produce the
control either. The arrangement actually measured is a latticed POCKET in a connected
solid, whose 2-voxel side walls carry load BY DESIGN: removing the cells there would
leave a perfectly good load path through the walls and the control would show nothing.
Stating this rather than leaving it unmentioned, per the reviewer. What stands in its
place is per-line evidence from the real runs: load_dropped 0.0000, zero_stress 0.0000,
members_dropped 0, p99 0.31-0.37 MPa — the lattice is carrying, measured, at every size.

A KNOWN LIMIT OF THE INSTRUMENT, to be carried forward:
A LATTICE-ONLY BRIDGE BETWEEN TWO SOLID BODIES IS REFUSED BEFORE THE SOLVE.
beam_network.cpp:1121 — "A component is real if a Dirichlet node sits on one of its
elements." Support is hex connectivity plus Dirichlet; the beams do not count. Two
solid regions joined only by a lattice leave the far one an unsupported island and
`kIslandRefuseFraction` stops the run. Any future Stepped certificate inherits this. It
does not affect a part whose lattice sits in a pocket inside a connected shell — which
is every real part, and why organic certifies — but it means core cannot today certify
a design whose load path is lattice-only between two bodies.
