## A plain TPU-to-rigid contact keeps a tenth to a fifth of TPU's strength

Every measured FDM interface between TPU and a rigid thermoplastic is far weaker than either material on its own. Five joint papers were read in full on 2026-10-24. Each one pulls apart parts co-printed on two heads, and none tests compression, repeated loading or separately printed parts. Dairabayeva, Perveen and Talamona printed seven interface designs between Tough PLA and TPU 95A in one dual-extrusion job on an Ultimaker S3 and pulled them in tension (Table 7; TPU alone is Table 2) ([Dairabayeva et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1108/RPJ-07-2022-0221)). The dovetail and U-shape failed by the TPU slipping out. The T-shape broke at its weakest section without separating. Overlap, where the layers alternate material over 3 mm, tied statistically with the T at 5.6 MPa and stretched by far the most before breaking (strain 2.0). **The 19.9 MPa encapsulation figure is not a joint strength.** That specimen broke near the grip with the TPU "almost intact", and the paper credits the Tough PLA casing, so it ranks no joint here. Ribeiro and colleagues found the same order for co-printed PLA-TPU under pull. The T beat the U and the dovetail, which slid apart while the T held until the plastic broke ([Ribeiro et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1108/RPJ-05-2017-0107)).

Dairabayeva, Ribeiro, Lopes and Tamburrino were read in full. Kuipers (ITIL, no TPU) is in the next section. The Xue, Brancewicz-Steinmetz, Harris and Jafor rows were not re-checked.

The rows come from different test types and cannot all be ranked against each other, but the PLA-TPU rows agree on four points. First, **test method matters as much as the joint.** The same flat co-printed joint measured 3.2 MPa in one study and 6.5 MPa in the other, so TopOpt's coupons must copy the app's exact settings. Second, for PLA-TPU **the interlock pattern and the print order matter more than temperature.** Tamburrino's slicer interlock pocket doubled plain lines, printing TPU first cut that to a quarter, and the authors found "the thermal aspect seems to be less significant" than the pattern. Finer layers did help: at 230 °C, 0.1 mm layers were **12 times stronger** than 0.3 mm layers, and printing 0.1 mm layers only in a 6 mm band around the interface raised strength **from 2.68 to 4.24 MPa (+58 %)** (Xue). Jafor's fivefold swing from heating the interface comes from a CF-nylon peel test, a different study type and rigid partner. Third, **the head switch alone costs 45 %.** Printing TPU on both sides of a head switch dropped it from 30.3 to 16.6 MPa with no change of material (Lopes). Fourth, **a key shape beats a slide-in shape**: the T beat the dovetail in both TPU studies.

**PLA is the only rigid partner tested against TPU in these papers. Nothing ranks PETG, PET or ABS against TPU.** The ABS, ASA and CF-PA rows above come from lap-shear and peel tests and are background only. For PETG with TPU, the most likely pairing on a Bambu machine, no peer-reviewed number exists. Bambu's wiki says the two "bond easily", but gives no data ([Bambu Lab wiki](https://wiki.bambulab.com/en/software/bambu-studio/beam-interlocking)). Dovetail, omega and T keys also improved ASA-to-TPU specimens ([Ermolai et al. 2024](https://www.mechanics-industry.org/articles/meca/abs/2024/01/mi230096/mi230096.html); not checked in full, a different rigid partner).

Two process facts bear directly on TopOpt's geometry. When the two materials sit side by side in the same layers (a vertical interface), the tool switch leaves a micro zig-zag interlock. When one material is stacked on the other (a horizontal interface, which is TopOpt's floor), the seam is smooth (Xue). And the rigid material should go down first. In Tamburrino's PLA-TPU butt joints, printing TPU first dropped the pocket joint from 1.00–1.32 to 0.28–0.32 MPa. The paper's advice is to print the rigid material first and fill solid below the interface (Sec. 5), and Brancewicz-Steinmetz's shear tests point the same way. The floor case prints in that favourable order, while a rigid lip or cap printed onto TPU prints in the weak one.

All of these numbers come from pull, shear or peel tests on co-printed parts. A squish part instead loads its joint mostly in compression and over many cycles, and a joint in compression needs no bond to carry load. Interface-aware topology optimization reaches the same conclusion: optimal multi-material designs place their interfaces mainly in compression ([Liu, Luo, Kang 2016](https://hit.globalimpact.cn/en/publications/multi-material-topology-optimization-considering-interface-behavi/)). The joint's real work is resisting peel at the perimeter of each face-prism, lateral shear from off-axis presses, and lift-off on rebound or handling. That shapes where joints go. The floor is the orientation with the weakest bond, but it is loaded in compression and can take a plain bonded plate. The side walls and the edge of the pressed face are loaded in peel and are where interlocks or anchors belong.

## Two heads: interlock a solid TPU plate, never the lattice walls

The multi-head route already has a research-backed tool, though its evidence is narrower than the slicers suggest. Kuipers, Su, Wu and Wang's ITIL design interlaces horizontal beams of the two materials and switches the beam direction from one layer group to the next. It keeps extrusion continuous where dovetails break it. With PP and Tough PLA it reached **7.07 ± 1.0 MPa, 82 % of a theoretical 8.6 MPa** (Sec. 4.2) ([Kuipers et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.addma.2021.102495)). Three limits matter. **No TPU was tested.** **Only vertical interfaces were tested**: non-vertical ones "remain future work", and TopOpt's floor is horizontal. **The paper contradicts itself on dovetails.** The abstract calls ITIL comparable to dovetails, while Sec. 4.3 says the dovetails performed considerably worse. Its parameters were set for 0.1 mm layers, with beams at least 2 layers tall and an interface depth of up to 3.6 mm. The design shipped as "Generate Interlocking Structure" in Cura 5.3. OrcaSlicer and Bambu Studio carry the same six settings with the same defaults. **These defaults are the slicers' own, not the paper's**: 0.8 mm beam width, 22.5° orientation, 2 layers per beam, a depth of 2 cells and a boundary avoidance of 2 cells, off by default ([Cura fdmprinter.def.json](https://github.com/Ultimaker/Cura/blob/main/resources/definitions/fdmprinter.def.json); [OrcaSlicer PrintConfig.cpp](https://github.com/SoftFever/OrcaSlicer/blob/main/src/libslic3r/PrintConfig.cpp); [BambuStudio PrintConfig.cpp](https://github.com/bambulab/BambuStudio/blob/master/src/libslic3r/PrintConfig.cpp)). PrusaSlicer's help pages, filed under 2.9, describe a beam interlock "based on OrcaSlicer's implementation" ([Prusa Help](https://help.prusa3d.com/article/mutliple-extruders-settings-interlocking-advanced-bonding_867124)). One research note found no PrusaSlicer interlock; the help article is the more direct evidence and is followed here. Bambu states the feature "only works within a single object", that is, between the parts of one object and not between separate objects ([Bambu Lab wiki](https://wiki.bambulab.com/en/software/bambu-studio/beam-interlocking)). A user also reports that it fires on PLA-to-PLA seams too and cannot be limited with a modifier ([Bambu forum](https://forum.bambulab.com/t/interlocking-beam-feature-how-to-only-target-specific-filaments/186348)).

The slicer feature is generic, and that clashes with TopOpt's lattice. CuraEngine builds the interlock on a voxel grid of 2 beam widths by 2 beam widths by 2 beam layers. It keeps only voxels that contain both meshes, and then **removes every voxel that also contains air, dilated by the boundary avoidance**, so the pattern never shows on the outside ([CuraEngine InterlockingGenerator.h](https://github.com/Ultimaker/CuraEngine/blob/main/include/InterlockingGenerator.h); [InterlockingGenerator.cpp](https://github.com/Ultimaker/CuraEngine/blob/main/src/InterlockingGenerator.cpp)). Two consequences follow from those steps; both are inferences and have not been tested in a slicer. First, a gyroid or honeycomb whose walls are 0.4 to 0.9 mm thick is mostly air at the scale of a 1.6 mm cell. Where its walls meet a rigid plate, most interface voxels contain air and are dropped, and a separate thin-area routine instead grows the meshes into each other. The interlock that results is patchy and unpredictable, and so is the lattice's local stiffness. Second, at default settings the band reaches 3.2 mm into each body at a side wall and about 1.6 mm at a floor printed with 0.2 mm layers. The no-interlock margin is also 3.2 mm. The owner's L-bracket has a 3 mm expand border (handoff :87, :1578), so the whole border sits inside that margin and would receive no interlock at all.

The design rule that follows is the most important single finding for this route: **end every lattice on a solid TPU interface plate, and interlock plate to rigid, never wall to rigid.** The plate is a solid sheet of whole beads that every lattice wall ends on, as thick as the interlock band or thicker. That way the beams sit in solid on both sides, and the bond area becomes the plate's whole face instead of the one-bead ends of the walls. The plate's side faces are the vertical interfaces ITIL was tested on; its floor face is not, so coupon set J must pull a floor plate too. TopOpt already owns most of the machinery for this. CORE LIST 13's seam-wall rule makes a solid wall that every lattice wall ends on (`FlexibleLatticeField.manualPieceWall` :455), and the Covered finish is already a solid TPU skin drawn through one distance channel (`FlexibleLatticeField.swift:23-45`). Minimum border thickness, which CORE LIST 10(b) already anticipates, then becomes a concrete rule: the border must be at least the interlock depth plus the boundary avoidance, or the interlock parameters must shrink to fit it.

The printer constraint is sharper than "multi-head" suggests. TopOpt's own filament catalogue marks varioShore `ams_compatible: false` (`core/src/materials/flexible_materials.json:31`), and its print notes say "No AMS for foaming TPU" (`04-print-settings.md:27`). The owner's X1C therefore cannot co-print varioShore with a rigid filament through the AMS. "Two heads" means a dual-nozzle machine or a tool changer only (his answer 7). Which models qualify was not verified. The export that keeps the slicer from guessing is **one 3MF object made of two parts**, rigid and TPU. The two meshes must be watertight, touch along one shared interface surface and not overlap. Overlap is resolved by a last-triangle-wins rule in the 3MF core and handled differently by each slicer ([3MF Core Specification](https://github.com/3MFConsortium/spec_core/blob/master/3MF%20Core%20Specification.md)). Each part carries its own extruder index in the slicer's metadata, which for Orca and Bambu is per-part metadata in `Metadata/model_settings.config` ([OrcaSlicer bbs_3mf.cpp](https://github.com/SoftFever/OrcaSlicer/blob/main/src/libslic3r/Format/bbs_3mf.cpp)). PrusaSlicer's equivalent file and Cura's handling of a two-part 3MF still have to be checked against current source. PrusaSlicer's "interface shells" should stay off, because it would add a solid skin the squish model does not know about. Three process settings each change the joint, so core writes each into the 3MF and the coupons are printed with exactly those values. The first is **the rigid side printed first, with solid fill below the interface** (Tamburrino, Sec. 5). The second is finer layers in a few-millimetre band around the interface (Xue). The third is a per-layer temperature schedule (CORE LIST 14) on the TPU tool only, which for PLA-TPU matters less than the pattern.

## Separate parts: four joints, and coupon set J is their only evidence

The separate-part route has no published test data at all. Every joint paper above pulls apart parts co-printed on two heads. None tests separately printed TPU and rigid parts, compression or repeated loading. **So for the owner's four separate-part joints, coupon set J is the only evidence, and every verdict below is an inference until it runs.** The co-printed failure modes are the best guide available. A dovetail or undercut holds TPU only while the TPU stays deformed inside it, and the same elasticity that let the TPU enter lets it leave. The dovetail and U specimens slid apart in both TPU studies, while the T held until the plastic broke (Dairabayeva; Ribeiro). Kuipers makes the same point from the other side: a dovetail can be "broken in plane by deformation", while a loop-through-loop lock fails only by fracture ([Kuipers et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.addma.2021.102495)). That is why the app should prefer the T-slot over the dovetail. It is also why a captured lip or loop should hold a soft part better than any slide-in shape, an inference coupon set J tests. Rigid T-heads pushed into a TPU socket hold only as well as the TPU resists stretching around them. When the TPU is printed around them by pause-and-insert, it grips them instead. Inkbit's patented two-material interlock offers a better anchor shape: closed-loop toroidal projections, or shafts with an enlarged tip trapped in a recess, with **berms at the roots to remove sharp corners** ([US 12049037 B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US12049037B2/en)). A T-slot should therefore get filleted roots.

Insert and capture both rely on a mid-print pause. Users add a pause (M601) or a filament-change command at the layer before a cavity closes, seat the insert in a press-fit pocket so the nozzle cannot drag it, and resume ([Prusa forum](https://forum.prusa3d.com/forum/how-do-i-print-this-printing-help/how-to-safely-insert-magnets-into-a-print)). The insert prints TPU over rigid, the favourable order. Capture prints rigid over a pre-printed TPU part, the order Tamburrino found weakest for a bond, so capture must hold by shape and not by bond. Overprinting in a second job is much weaker. The substrate is cold, flat PLA-to-TPU contact already measures only about 0.4 MPa in shear ([Brancewicz-Steinmetz 2021](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC8585249/fullTextXML)), and the nozzle cannot fill any undercut from above. Adhesives fail for reasons particular to this product. A lattice touches rigid only along wall edges, cyanoacrylate fills gaps of only about 0.1 to 0.15 mm ([Loctite 401 guide](https://aimsindustrial.com.au/en-nz/blogs/product-guides/loctite-401-guide)), a glue fillet stiffens the first cells by an unknown amount, and the result depends on the user's preparation.

Hard design rules are thin. The only stated rule for interlock feature size is **at least 2 to 3 extrusion widths across**, and it comes from a practitioner, not a test ([3D Print Decoded](https://3dprintdecoded.com/articles/printing-tpu-and-pla-together/)). Generic FDM clearance runs 0.2 to 0.5 mm ([Zbotic tolerances guide](https://zbotic.in/3d-printing-tolerances-designing-gaps-for-press-fits-threads-and-snap-fits/)), and sources disagree about TPU press fits. TopOpt should therefore treat clearance as a calibrated value from a fit coupon, not a constant. It should size anchors by strength times neck area with a large safety factor, then confirm them with a pull-out coupon. Both routes share one structural point: **a lattice cannot hold a dovetail or an anchor; a solid TPU plate can.** The interface plate from the multi-head route is the same object that carries the rail, the flange, the insert or the captured lip here. A separate part also brings something co-printing does not: its seating clearance is dead travel before any force builds, so the force-depth curve starts with an offset.

## The joint moves the column, the anvil and the curve, so core has to design it

The owner's guess is right. The reasons are mechanical and they are also already TopOpt policy. DECISIONS 2026-09-27 #4 puts the single definition of Flexible's printable geometry in core, with preview and export drawn from the same functions (`docs/DECISIONS.md:189-192`). DECISIONS #6 says TopOpt does not slice (`:197-199`). Together they mean the joint must be core-made geometry plus a per-body material assignment, never toolpaths. Today none of it exists. Core's Flexible job refuses a second material outright, with the message "a Flexible job names one filament" (`core/src/flexible/job_block.cpp:86-88`). The app's squish sim treats every non-lattice voxel as solid TPU at varioShore's 50 MPa (`flexible_squish_fe.cpp:176-181`, `:264-278`). Stress and Topology treat that same solid as a hidden ABS (D-R3-17; CORE LIST 8(b)); no paper checked ranks ABS against TPU. Picking a print route is what settles this existing contradiction.

The squish design rests on assumptions that a joint breaks. Core sets each column's height to its latticed length (`field.cpp:290`, `c.height_mm = sc.lattice_mm`) and holds each column at the far side of the part (`faces.cpp:291-309`). With a joint, the interface band and the solid plate are not lattice: the working height becomes the latticed length minus the bands minus the skins. That is the same subtraction `squish.cpp:14-20` already promises for skins. A floor joint becomes the column's real anvil, which is CORE LIST 5's bounded column. The band sits **in series** with the lattice (CORE LIST 18): rigid interlock teeth are effectively rigid, a solid TPU plate is near-rigid but bends, and a separate insert's clearance is dead travel. Bonded side walls also restrain the lattice next to them. In the app's sim of the owner's L-bracket, the centre already squishes 11.7 times as far as the rim (CORE LIST 10). Independent columns cannot see this (CORE LIST 30).

The coupon data carry a hidden boundary assumption. Every curve row comes from coupons squished between lubricated platens, with 0.8 mm TPU skins and no side skin (`docs/design/flexibles/data/coupon_sets.json:8-17`). Lattice mechanics says a bonded face sheet stiffens the cells next to it until about **5 cells per edge** in one metal-lattice study ([IYTE handle 11147/12153](https://openaccess.iyte.edu.tr/handle/11147/12153?mode=full)), and size effects persist to **8 to 12 cells** in reviews ([Yang, SFF 2016](https://utw10945.utweb.utexas.edu/sites/default/files/2016/174-Yang.pdf)). TopOpt's flexible layers are often only a few cells deep, so the whole lattice can sit inside the boundary-affected zone. A product bonded or clamped to rigid will be stiffer than its lubricated-platen design by an amount nobody has measured.

The multi-material topology-optimization literature points the same way. Designs that assume a perfect bond risk debonding failure ([Liu, Luo, Kang 2016](https://hit.globalimpact.cn/en/publications/multi-material-topology-optimization-considering-interface-behavi/)). Modelling a graded interface reveals higher true peak stress than ignoring it ([Han et al., NWPU](https://pure.nwpu.edu.cn/en/publications/stress-based-multi-material-structural-topology-optimization-cons/)). Interface location becomes a design outcome once the joint has its own material tensor and stress limit ([Zhou, Nomura, Saitou 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10393)). The commercial lattice tools that were checked (nTop, Fusion, Carbon, Altair, 3-matic) handle the lattice-to-solid join only within one material. None of them was found to model a bonded rigid-to-elastomer joint, so nothing exists to copy.

The best argument for an export-only approach comes from the tools research. On a multi-head printer, the minimum viable product is two touching volumes on two extruders, with the slicer's beam interlock switched on. That fails on three counts. The slicer interlock cannot see the lattice and is patchy on thin walls. Its band changes the working height and stiffness that core designed for. And the export still needs core: a body split, a second object, and a fix for `keep_largest_component` (`mesh/mesh.cpp:653`), which would delete one of the two bodies. The writers are also single-object today (`core/src/io/threemf.cpp:22,59`; `threemf_stream.cpp:124,191-192`). The literature's split by scale is the right one. Everything that changes stiffness, load path or the designed squish belongs in core's model: the route, the body split, the plate and band, the hold type and the frozen rigid anchor zone. The fine tooth profile could in principle be generated at export from parameters core has reserved, but under DECISIONS #4 even that lives in core's exporter, not the slicer.

The rigid-side physics adds one more reason the work belongs in core and cannot be done cheaply. Rigid PLA or PETG against lattice moduli as low as about 0.03 MPa is a stiffness contrast of roughly 10⁴ to 10⁵ (a stiffness point only: PLA is the only one of the two with TPU joint data). Core's multigrid already stalls at 220× (CORE LIST 38). Where the rigid body is anchored, holding the solid beyond the interface, as the bridge's `FlexHeldWall` already does, is the practical route. Only a compliant rigid frame needs a full two-material solve.

The recommended approach follows the owner's rule: front end first, back end after, with sends to core held off and interim sims labelled. In the app, the route picker ("How will you print it?" Under Two parts the joint is one of his four: insert, dovetail or T-slot, flange, or capture. For the dovetail or T-slot, the app recommends places, preferring the T-slot, and the user may place it by hand. The Covered skin and the Rim band get a material dropdown (TPU or rigid, default TPU), and the preview and the sim show the pick. Walls that `FlexibleLatticeWalls` already classifies as `thin` are refused for anchors in one line with a one-tap fix. The preview gains one more distance channel for the plate and a material colour, in parity with its Metal twin. The sim's held walls gain a sliding hold kind for separate parts, reusing D-R5-G3's normal-only hold, and non-lattice voxels take their body's modulus. The export card lists the files each route will produce and stays disabled. The job's bytes do not change.

Core's order then follows its dependencies. 52 (route and rigid material) comes first. 53 (body split) waits on CORE LIST 1 and 9(b), because a seam rod or Balanced's empty top voxel layer is cosmetic with one material but becomes a hole or a 0.21 mm rigid sheet with two. Coupon set J runs in parallel on his squish rig, with the rig's own plates printed with the same interface and the app's exact settings. The co-printed coupons are a flat face-to-face baseline, the slicer interlock over a solid plate at a side wall and at the floor, overlap, and rigid-first against TPU-first. The separate-part coupons are the insert, the T-slot beside the dovetail, the flange and the capture. Lattice against lubricated skins is also covered. The tests are pull-off, peel, pull-out, compression and cyclic.

What the paper check changes:
- **Joint (b):** the place recommender prefers the T-slot over the dovetail. The T beat the dovetail in both TPU studies.
- **Joint (d), capture:** a captured lip or loop should hold a soft part better than a slide-in shape. That is an inference, and coupon set J tests it.
- **Two heads:** the rigid side prints first, with solid fill below the interface. This is a setting core writes into the 3MF beside the interlock settings.
- **Overlap** (layers alternating material over 3 mm) joins coupon set J as a co-print coupon. It tied with the T and stretched the most.
- **Test method matters:** the two flat PLA-TPU studies disagree (3.2 against 6.5 MPa). The coupons therefore copy the app's exact settings, and published numbers never stand in for them.

For v1 of the multi-head route, the defensible position is that core owns the plate, the band, the body split and the two-body export. It writes the slicer's beam-interlock settings and the rigid-first, solid-below order into the 3MF project explicitly, and the coupons are printed with exactly those settings. Core-drawn interlock teeth come only if coupon set J shows the slicer's interlock varies too much (his answer 3). With identical defaults across four slicers and a solid plate for the beams to live in, the slicer no longer decides the joint: it carries out a recipe that core stated and the coupons measured. Core-drawn ITIL beams would also have to line up with the slicer's layer grid, a new coupling that macroscopic keys avoid. For separate parts, v1 offers his four joints: Insert (the app generates the insert and the pause layer on export), Dovetail or T-slot (recommended places, hand placement allowed), Flange (under a rigid lip or a screwed ring) and Capture (the app generates the capturing lip or a separate slide-in retainer). ## Conclusion

The idea that ties the two routes together is the solid TPU interface plate. The owner's question reads as two different joint problems, but both reduce to one geometry decision that TopOpt already knows how to draw. The co-print interlock needs solid TPU for its beams. The dovetail or T-slot, the flange, the insert and the capture all need a solid TPU body to grip. And the plate turns a lattice's scattered bead-end contacts into a measurable bonded face. Once the plate exists, choosing a route also resolves a contradiction already in the codebase: the sim's solid is TPU while Stress and Topology's solid is ABS. The question also quietly un-defers CORE LIST 49. "Two heads" is the never-built Mixed route and "Two parts" is the Cut route, so per-piece materials stop being hypothetical.

The second takeaway concerns honesty more than geometry. Not one published test loads a TPU-to-rigid FDM joint the way a squish part does: in compression and cycles, as separately printed parts, through a lattice rather than solid TPU, with a foaming filament, or with any rigid partner but PLA. Every joint paper is a pull test on co-printed parts, and even those disagree twofold on the same flat joint. So the printable product is a stated joint recipe plus coupons that copy it, not a predicted joint strength. For the four separate-part joints, the coupons are the only evidence there is. That fits DECISIONS #1's rule of notices, never margins. It also makes the owner's nearly finished squish rig the critical path. Until rig plates printed with the real interface produce joint-matched curves, any squish TopOpt designs for a bonded or clamped lattice will come out stiffer in the real part than designed, by an amount nobody has measured.
